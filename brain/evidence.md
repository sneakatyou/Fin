## The short version

1. The best-replicated edges (momentum, value, profitability, trend following) are slow, diversified and modest. None of it speaks to making Rs 10k-50k in a day.
2. Published edges shrink. McLean and Pontiff found predictor returns 26% lower out of sample and 58% lower after publication. Expect live results well below any backtest.
3. Momentum crashes cluster after market declines, in sharp rebounds (Daniel and Moskowitz). NIFTY is up about 4.4% off its 22,179.90 low, which looks like that state. It is a caution flag for momentum-style shorts, not a forecast.
4. Earnings drift was real but has faded in large stocks (Martineau 2022; contested). India evidence is mixed. Surprise plus revisions plus price trend is better supported than any one alone.
5. Intraday evidence is thin. Market intraday momentum rests on one US paper with no verified India test. Opening-range breakout rests on practitioner working papers, and an independent replication found nothing after costs.
6. The base rate for individuals is harsh. SEBI reports about 71% of intraday cash traders lost money in FY23, and about 91% of F&O individuals in FY25 and 88% in FY26. Taiwan data agree.
7. India factors (IIMA, 1994 to Dec 2025): momentum about 10.7% a year, value 6.6%, size -3.7%. Momentum fell about 62% in 2000, and two vintages of the library differ by 9 points on momentum.
8. Promoter share pledging is linked to higher crash risk and weaker performance in Indian firms. This bears on a book that is 57% one pledged group.
9. Index-inclusion pops in India are mostly short-lived; in the US they have almost disappeared.
10. Indian FII flows follow returns more than they lead them, so heavy FII selling describes the tape rather than predicting it.
11. Seven setups and zero trades say nothing. Confirming a +0.2R edge takes about 330 trades, and +0.1R over 600.
12. Thirteen hypotheses tested at once give about a 26% chance that one looks significant by luck alone.

## How to read this page

- This is research for Shashank's own decisions, not advice, and it recommends no trades.
- Each citation was checked by web search against an abstract, library record or reliable summary. I read no paper in full. Items tagged (NV) are from memory, unchecked because the search budget ran out; I attribute no result to them beyond their topic.
- **Strong:** replicated by independent authors across samples or markets, plausible mechanism, survives realistic retail costs. **Moderate:** replicated, but with known decay, narrow samples or cost fragility. **Weak:** one study, practitioner-only work, or the effect vanishes after costs. **Contested:** credible authors disagree. These are my judgements, not a published scale.
- **India status:** Holds, Mixed, Not found (no verified study) or Anecdotal.

## 1. Cross-sectional factors

### Value (Fama and French)

- **Found:** Fama and French (1992, Journal of Finance 47(2)) found beta had little power to explain average returns, while size and book-to-market did; Fama and French (1993, Journal of Financial Economics 33) built the three-factor model (SMB, HML). Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen (2013, Journal of Finance 68(3), "Value and Momentum Everywhere") found value premia in eight markets and asset classes, negatively correlated with momentum.
- **Strength:** Moderate. It replicates widely, but payoffs take years and have long dry spells.
- **India:** Holds, in sample. BSE studies find a positive premium (1996-2010; Tripathi and Aggarwal 2021, 1999-2017, who attribute it mainly to size, liquidity and institutional ownership; Sharma, Srikanth and Mathew 2022, 17 of 21 industries). IIMA's HML is about 6.6% a year since 1994.
- **For us:** Valuation dislocation and Valuation Rotation Candidate. The premium is an average over hundreds of stocks, including many value traps. Cheap alone is not the evidence.

### Momentum (Jegadeesh and Titman)

- **Found:** Jegadeesh and Titman (1993, Journal of Finance 48(1)) showed that buying 3-12 month winners and selling losers earned significant returns over 3-12 month holds, and that part of the first-year profit dissipates over the next two years. Summaries put the original profit near 1% a month; I did not re-check that. Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen (2013) found momentum premia in the same eight markets.
- **Strength:** Strong as a diversified, monthly-rebalanced portfolio. Weak as a basis for trading one or two names.
- **India:** Holds, with violent drawdowns. Dhankar and Maheshwari (NSE, 1997-2013) found short-term momentum and long-term overreaction; Garg and Varshney (Global Business Review, 2015) found sector momentum robust over 2000-2013. IIMA's WML is about 10.65% a year since 1994, with a worst drawdown of roughly 62-63% starting March 2000 (the library advises survivorship-adjusted values for 1995-2000) and about 53% in 2008-09.
- **For us:** Sector-wave momentum and Earnings/revision momentum.

### Momentum crashes (Daniel and Moskowitz)

- **Found:** Daniel and Moskowitz (2016, Journal of Financial Economics 122(2)) show momentum earns strong average returns but suffers rare, severe crashes, partly forecastable, clustered in panic states: after market declines, when volatility is high, and in market rebounds. After a decline the portfolio is effectively short high-beta losers, so a sharp rebound hurts. Scaling exposure to forecast momentum variance roughly doubled alpha and Sharpe versus static momentum (NBER abstract). Barroso and Santa-Clara (2015, Journal of Financial Economics) report a related volatility-scaling result (NV).
- **Strength:** Strong that crashes exist and cluster in these states. **India:** Holds in the IIMA drawdowns above; the rebound mechanism is untested there.
- **For us:** Buying after sharp falls and shorting after declines are two sides of this state. The research is about stock portfolios, not NIFTY intraday trades, so transfer is untested. The usable lesson is that post-decline rebounds can be fast, which supports the rule that a gap through a level is not a reclaim.

### Profitability and quality

- **Found:** Novy-Marx (2013, Journal of Financial Economics 108(1)) found gross profits-to-assets predicts returns about as well as book-to-market, and adding profitability to value helps, especially in large liquid stocks. Asness, Frazzini and Pedersen (2019, Review of Accounting Studies 24(1), "Quality Minus Junk") define quality as profitability, growth and safety and find quality stocks earn higher risk-adjusted returns while only modestly pricier.
- **Strength:** Moderate to Strong. Definitions of quality vary.
- **India:** Not found. The IIMA library has no quality factor.
- **For us:** A check on Valuation dislocation: cheap without profitability is the classic value trap.

### Low volatility and betting against beta

- **Found:** Frazzini and Pedersen (2014, Journal of Financial Economics 111(1)) argue leverage-constrained investors bid up high-beta assets, so beta and alpha are inversely related, across US stocks, 20 other equity markets, bonds and futures. Novy-Marx and Velikov argue the returns come mainly from non-standard beta estimation and tilts toward profitability and investment.
- **Strength:** Contested. **India:** Not found.
- **For us:** The mandate accepts volatility deliberately. If BAB is right, high beta has not historically been paid on a risk-adjusted basis, and much of his book is probably high-beta (for example Suzlon, IREDA, AI hardware; I have not measured betas). Each such position needs a specific thesis, not "beta is rewarded".

### Size

- **Found:** SMB comes from Fama and French (1993). Hou, Xue and Zhang show many anomalies concentrate in microcaps (next section).
- **Strength:** Weak to Contested in recent decades.
- **India:** Contrary. IIMA's SMB is about -3.7% a year since 1994, with a worst drawdown near 86-87% (1995 to March 2009). A 2017 study (Global Business and Economics Review) found large caps beat small caps.
- **For us:** No India evidence that small size is paid. Small-cap risk is a stock thesis, not a factor.

## 2. Is the factor zoo real? Replication, multiple testing and decay

- **Hurdle:** Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016, Review of Financial Studies 29(1)) argue that, given the many factors tried, a new factor needs a t-statistic above 3.0, not 2.0.
- **Failures:** Hou, Xue and Zhang (2020, Review of Financial Studies 33(5), "Replicating Anomalies") re-examined 452 anomalies. With microcaps mitigated (NYSE breakpoints, value-weighting), 65% fail an absolute t of 1.96 and 82% fail a multiple-testing hurdle of 2.78; survivors' magnitudes are much smaller than first reported. Secondary summaries say survivors include value, momentum, investment and profitability.
- **Rebuttals:** Chen and Zimmermann (2022, Critical Finance Review 11(2)) reproduced 319 predictors; 98% of the 161 clearly significant ones had t above 1.96. Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen (2023, Journal of Finance 78(5)) find most factors replicate, group into 13 themes and hold out of sample in 93 countries.
- **Decay:** McLean and Pontiff (2016, Journal of Finance) studied 97 predictors: returns 26% lower out of sample (an upper bound on data mining) and 58% lower post-publication, the 32-point gap read as publication-informed trading. Declines were larger for predictors with higher in-sample returns.
- **Strength:** Strong that decay and data mining are real; Contested on how many published factors are false. **India:** Not found. None is an India test.
- **For us:** Presume any pattern from a chart, blog or backtest is one draw from the zoo. A reasonable bar is t above 3 on out-of-sample data, and a haircut of half or more to published edges. The famous few are better supported than the long tail.

## 3. Earnings and revisions

- **Found:** Ball and Brown (1968, Journal of Accounting Research 6(2)) ran the first earnings event study and found returns kept drifting after announcements. Bernard and Thomas (1989, Journal of Accounting Research 27, Supplement) found the drift favoured delayed price response over a risk premium. Chan, Jegadeesh and Lakonishok (1996, Journal of Finance 51(5)) found past returns and past earnings surprises each predict large drifts after controlling for the other, and that analysts' forecasts adjust sluggishly. Their 1999 Financial Analysts Journal follow-up found strategies on returns, surprises and consensus revisions profitable at intermediate horizons, with costs a key concern given turnover.
- **Decay:** Martineau (2022, Critical Finance Review 11(3-4), "Rest in Peace Post-Earnings Announcement Drift") finds no drift in large stocks since 2006 and, recently, none in microcaps. A UCLA Anderson brief says two later accepted papers contradict him.
- **Strength:** Strong historically; Contested today. Revision momentum: Moderate (Gleason and Lee 2003, The Accounting Review (NV)).
- **India:** Mixed. A University of Nottingham event study found efficient reaction to good news but a negative drift of over a week after bad news, mostly in the 2009-10 bull market. An IIMB repository record reports drift over 120 days. Gupta and Dhusia (2021; 100 NSE firms, 2014-18) found prices adjust properly. Abstracts only.
- **For us:** Earnings/revision momentum. The rationale is well documented, but the cleanest version, large-cap drift after the announcement day, is the one most in doubt. The better-supported edge is surprise, revisions and trend together, which matches the failure mode "momentum without revisions".

## 4. Time-series momentum and trend following

- **Found:** Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen (2012, Journal of Financial Economics 104(2)) studied 58 liquid futures and found returns persist for 1-12 months and partly reverse later; a diversified portfolio earned abnormal returns and did best in extreme markets. Hurst, Ooi and Pedersen (2017, Journal of Portfolio Management 44(1)) report positive trend returns across 67 markets and four asset classes from 1880 to 2016, with diversification in equity bear markets (working-paper figures). A Quantpedia summary says critics find the alpha falls from 1.27% to 0.41% without volatility scaling.
- **Strength:** Moderate to Strong for diversified, volatility-scaled futures trend following; Weak for a single market. **India:** Not found.
- **For us:** The evidence is for dozens of markets, monthly or longer signals and volatility targeting. It does not support an intraday single-index rule such as Support-breakdown continuation. It bears more on the long-only, levered pledged book: trend rules helped in equity bear markets, a candidate for the monthly review.

## 5. Short horizon

### Short-term reversal and liquidity

- **Found:** Lehmann (1990, Quarterly Journal of Economics 105(1)) found weekly winners and losers reverse the next week, with profits that appeared to survive spreads and plausible costs. Jegadeesh (1990, Journal of Finance) documented monthly reversal (details unverified). Heston, Korajczyk and Sadka (2010, Journal of Finance 65(4)) tie short-run reversal to liquidity imbalances under an hour and bid-ask bounce, and found returns repeat at the same half-hour of later days for about 40 trading days (NYSE, 2001-2005). Nagel (2012, Review of Financial Studies) links reversal to liquidity provision that pays most when volatility is high (NV).
- **Strength:** Moderate for individual stocks; Weak after retail costs. **India:** Not found.
- **For us:** Oversold index reversal. The evidence is stock-level, not index-level, and rewards providing liquidity into stress. It does not show that "it fell, so it bounces" works, which is a named failure mode.

### Overnight versus intraday

- **Found:** Lou, Polk and Skouras (2019, Journal of Financial Economics 134(1), "A Tug of War") found returns persist within the overnight and intraday components but are offset by cross-period reversal. Across 14 strategies, profits came almost wholly from one window, usually with opposite signs; momentum profits accrue overnight. Berkman, Koch, Tuttle and Zhang (2012, Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis 47(4)) found high overnight returns in recently high-attention stocks tend to reverse during the day because retail demand lifts opening prices; implicit costs of buying near the open often exceeded the half-spread.
- **Strength:** Moderate (US, plausible mechanism).
- **India:** Anecdotal. The popular claim that most of Nifty's long-run gain comes overnight is unverified (blog summaries I could not trace to data). NSE studies find higher variance in the day than overnight and an open-jump and end-of-session pattern, but no mean split.
- **For us:** Gap handling and Post-capitulation gap-and-go. Both papers lean against buying a gap at the open on average, consistent with SETUP-IN-0001.

### Intraday momentum

- **Found:** Gao, Han, Li and Zhou (2018, Journal of Financial Economics, pp. 394-414, "Market Intraday Momentum") found the first half-hour return, measured from the prior close, predicts the last half-hour return in S&P 500 ETF data, 1993-2013. It is stronger on volatile, high-volume, recession and macro-news days, and appears in ten other ETFs. Liu and Tse (2017) report that for index futures the first half-hour is not predictive but the overnight return predicts the last half-hour.
- **Strength:** Moderate for US index ETFs; Weak elsewhere. **India:** Not found. No Nifty test turned up; a study of Indonesian stocks found a similar first-to-last pattern.
- **For us:** Intraday momentum gate, the closest research. Two cautions: the signal includes the overnight gap, so it is not purely intraday, and the effect is small against round-trip costs. Test it on his own Nifty minute data before live use.

### Opening-range breakout

- **Found:** Zarattini and Aziz (SSRN 4416622, April 2023, revised September 2025, "Can Day Trading Really Be Profitable?") test an opening-range breakout in QQQ for 2016-2023 and argue it beats buy-and-hold when leveraged. Zarattini, Barbon and Aziz (SSRN 4729284, February 2024) test a 5-minute breakout on over 7,000 US stocks, limited to unusually active "stocks in play"; a Concretum summary of the authors' claims gives a Sharpe of 2.81 for a top-20 portfolio. An independent blog replication on five index CFDs (2015-2026) reproduced gross returns but found nothing distinguishable from zero after costs, noting the paper charges commission but no spread or slippage and has no out-of-sample period.
- **Strength:** Weak. Practitioner-affiliated working papers over one benign window; I could not retrieve results by range length (they compare 5, 15, 30 and 60 minutes). **India:** Not found.
- **For us:** 15-min opening-range breakout. The 7,000-stock result depends on selecting news-driven, high-volume stocks, a different object from a breakout on NIFTY.

### Gaps and transaction costs

- **Gaps:** I found no peer-reviewed study of gap continuation versus fill for Indian stocks or indices. "Nifty always fills its gaps" claims are trader posts with no sample sizes: Anecdotal. The Berkman and Lou papers are the nearest evidence.
- **Costs:** Novy-Marx and Velikov (2016, Review of Financial Studies 29(1)) find most anomalies with under 50% monthly turnover still earn significant net spreads when designed to cut costs, and few above that do. They use effective spreads, which understate costs for larger orders. Strength: Strong as a principle.
- **For us:** Intraday strategies turn capital over many times a day, far above the 50% monthly line, so costs are the first thing to test. I did not verify current Indian brokerage and tax rates; use contract notes and record cost per round trip in R.

## 6. Who makes money intraday

### Taiwan and US broker data

- **Found:** Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean (2009, Review of Financial Studies 22(2), "Just how much do individual investors lose by trading?") used all Taiwan Stock Exchange trades, 1995-1999. Individuals underperformed by about 3.8 points a year, losses equalled about 2.2% of GDP, institutions earned about 1.5 points above the market, and costs were just under two-thirds of the loss. Barber, Lee, Liu, Odean and Zhang (2020, Review of Asset Pricing Studies 10(1), "Learning, Fast or Slow") study Taiwan day traders, 1992-2006: aggregate performance is negative, and a working-paper abstract says about 97% are likely to lose in future day trading. Barber and Odean (2000, Journal of Finance 55(2), "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth") studied 66,465 US households (1991-1996): the most active earned 11.4% a year against 17.9% for the market.
- **Disposition effect:** Odean (1998, Journal of Finance 53(5)) studied about 10,000 accounts and found investors sell winners more readily than losers, unjustified by later returns.
- **Strength:** Strong.
- **For us:** Hold-for-thesis is where the disposition effect bites: a thesis can become a reason not to realise a loss. It supports logging rejected-candidate outcomes whether or not they hurt.

## 7. India-specific

### IIMA factor library

- **Found:** Agarwalla, Jacob and Varma (IIM Ahmedabad Working Paper 2013-09-05, "Four factor model in Indian equities market") built market, size, value and momentum factors from CMIE Prowess data, excluding illiquid firms and correcting for survivorship. The library was relaunched in December 2021 after the legacy series was suspended in 2020. The 2025-12 release gives annualised geometric returns since January 1994 of about 4.93% (market premium), -3.71% (SMB), 6.61% (HML) and 10.65% (WML). The legacy page (data to December 2019) showed WML at 19.3%.
- **Strength:** Moderate as description, Weak as a forecast. Factor numbers depend on vintage and universe.
- **For us:** India has paid for momentum and value, not for small size, and momentum has had a 60%-plus drawdown. That favours the slow families (Valuation dislocation, Earnings/revision momentum, Sector-wave momentum) over intraday ones, with sizing set for deep drawdowns.

### FII flows and Indian returns

- **Found:** Studies point mostly toward returns driving flows. Inoue (2008, IDE-JETRO discussion paper 180; 1999-2008) found one-way causality from returns to flows, with reverse causality only after 2003. Guru and Parikh (2009; 2003-2008) found two-way causality but larger effects from returns to flows. Chandra (2012) and Paliwal and Vashishtha (2011) report similar results. Samples mostly end around 2010.
- **Strength:** Weak to Moderate; Contested on causality. **India:** Mixed.
- **For us:** Heavy FII selling and DII buying describes what the tape did. The evidence does not support using it as a trigger.

### Promoter share pledging

- **Found:** Kalia (2024, Journal of Advances in Management Research; 257 BSE 500 firms, 2011-2020) finds pledging linked to higher future crash risk and weaker financial performance, robust to an instrumental-variable test. Chauhan, Mishra and Spahr (2021, Financial Management; 1,452 firms) find large pledges raise volatility, lower firm value and hurt longer-term performance.
- **Strength:** Moderate; consistent but observational. **India:** Holds.
- **For us:** Check promoter pledge levels for every holding, especially the Vedanta group names in the collateral. I have not looked up any current figure. The research mechanism is margin-call pressure turning a price drop into forced selling, which his Rs 25 lakh margin against pledged stock shares at his own scale. See Collateral and Stress Test.

### Index inclusion

- **Found:** Indian evidence is mixed. Parthasarathy (2010; Nifty 1999-2010) found permanent positive abnormal returns around inclusion. Chhatwani (2000-2016) found gains reversing within about 60 days. Joshipura and Janakiramanan (2015) found an effective-day gain that did not last. In the US, Greenwood and Sammon (2025, Journal of Finance 80(2), "The Disappearing Index Effect") report the S&P 500 addition effect fell from about 7.4% in the 1990s to under 1% in the latest decade.
- **Strength:** Moderate for a brief price-pressure effect; Weak as an edge.
- **For us:** Catalyst swing and Event-driven special situation. Index-change news is a short, crowded event; a catalyst alone is not an edge.

### PSU versus private banks

- **Found:** I found no peer-reviewed study linking the public-sector discount to ownership, efficiency or returns. A Value Research piece (June 2026) says the Nifty PSU Bank index roughly doubled in three years and PSBs still trade near 1x book against 1.6x for private banks, arguing the gap may no longer be justified as NPAs are broadly comparable. Angel One put the average gap at 1.65x price-to-book from July 2022 to February 2024 and credits profitability. Business Standard reported in 2017 that the discount widened in the bad-loan crisis and narrowed after recapitalisation.
- **Strength:** Anecdotal.
- **For us:** Indian Bank is 23% of the Indian book and part of the collateral. Part of the re-rating has happened, and what remains is a credit-cycle and NPA judgement, not a documented factor.

## 8. Methodology: backtests, overfitting and sample size

### What the literature says

- Bailey and Lopez de Prado (2014, Journal of Portfolio Management 40(5), "The Deflated Sharpe Ratio") correct a Sharpe ratio for selection bias from multiple trials and for non-normal returns.
- Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado and Zhu (2017, Journal of Computational Finance 20(4), "The Probability of Backtest Overfitting") estimate the chance a chosen backtest is overfit, and argue simple hold-out samples are unreliable here.
- Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado and Zhu (2014, Notices of the AMS, "Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism") introduce a minimum backtest length. In a talk by Lopez de Prado, after about seven configurations one can expect a two-year backtest with an annualised Sharpe above 1 even when the true Sharpe is zero.
- **For us:** Each family is a trial. Record the variants tried per family (entry offsets, range lengths, filters), because the deflated Sharpe needs that count and retrofitting is a named failure mode.

### Why seven setups say nothing

These are my own calculations, not from a paper. They assume independent trades scored in R (a loss is 1R) with the win rate and payoff stated, a normal approximation, and Monte Carlo runs of 40,000 per case.

- A setup with a 40% win rate and 2R winners has true expectancy +0.20R and per-trade standard deviation 1.47R.
- After 7 trades the standard error of the average is 0.56R, so the 95% interval is about plus or minus 1.1R. A true +0.20R and a true -0.90R both fit.
- With no edge at all (40% win rate, 1.5R winners), a 7-trade sample shows a positive average 58% of the time and an average of at least +0.2R 29% of the time. That falls to 25% at 20 trades, 16% at 50, 6% at 100 and 1% at 200.
- Losing runs mislead too. At a 40% win rate, at least one run of 6 straight losses happens 43% of the time in 30 trades and 87% in 100.
- With 13 independent families each with no edge, the chance at least one clears t above 2 (one-sided) is about 26%, and the best of 13 random t-statistics averages about 1.7. His families are not independent, so this is approximate.
- Skeptical-prior illustration: take a true edge centred on zero with a spread of 0.1R and a per-trade SD of 1.47R. If 20 trades average +0.5R, the updated estimate is only about +0.04R.

## Proposals for the weekly review

Proposals only. No rule is changed.

1. Store an outside-evidence rating (the hypothesis table) as the prior on each family, so none is promoted from its own small sample alone.
2. Separate kill from promote decisions. About 30 trades can flag a process problem (slippage, rule breaks, costs) but cannot promote a family, which needs roughly 150 or more trades for a large edge.
3. Keep logging rejected candidates with what price did next. They add signal samples without capital at risk, but they are hypothetical and should stay apart from live P&L.
4. Record round-trip cost in R on every trade and count the variants tried per family, so a deflated Sharpe can be computed later.
5. Before live use, test the Intraday momentum gate and the 15-min opening-range breakout on his own Nifty minute data with a rule fixed in advance and a later period left untouched.
6. Add promoter pledge level and governance flags for each holding to the monthly review.
