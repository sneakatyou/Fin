## The short version
- **Price is not value, and the gap is the edge.** Buy only with a margin of safety measured against a conservative estimate of what owners can actually take out (Graham, Buffett, Klarman).
- **Risk is permanent loss, not volatility.** With margin against pledged stock, a temporary fall can be turned into a permanent loss by a forced sale (Marks, Klarman).
- **Ask what the price already implies before arguing about the business.** Write down the growth, margins and years the price needs (Mauboussin, Marks).
- **Know the category before you set the exit.** A compounder, a cyclical and a turnaround need different sell rules; most of his Indian book looks cyclical (Lynch).
- **Quality compounders are held, not traded, but a quality screen with no price check is how you overpay** (Fisher, Munger, Mukherjea versus Agrawal's "P" in QGLP).
- **Special situations pay for being on the other side of forced or uninterested sellers.** The spin-off evidence is mixed and the Indian long-run evidence is thin (Greenblatt, Klarman).
- **Concentration is only as good as the independence of the bets.** His 13 stocks are closer to five or six bets, and two clusters are also the collateral (Pabrai, Fisher, Damani).
- **Sell for the three Fisher reasons, plus price and cycle for non-compounders.** His "Valuation Rotation Candidate" label belongs mainly to Lynch, Graham and Marks territory (Fisher, Lynch, Marks).
- **In India, check the promoter first:** pledges, upstreaming of cash, related parties. Studies link pledging to crash risk (Kedia, Bakshi, pledge studies).
- **Cost and humility are the counterweight.** Most active large-cap funds in India lag their benchmark after costs; measure his books against an index (Bogle, SPIVA).
- **Cycle position matters even though cycle timing cannot be forecast** (Marks, Templeton).
- **Temperament is most of the game.** Ignore Mr Market's mood, invert, and be willing to do nothing (Graham, Munger).

## 1. What a business is worth, and margin of safety
**Graham.** *The Intelligent Investor* (1949, revised several times) and *Security Analysis* (1934, with David Dodd) set the frame. Three ideas carry through everything after them.
- **Investor versus speculator.** An investment operation, after thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and an adequate return. Anything else is speculation. Graham did not ban speculation; as I recall Chapter 1, he asked that it be recognised as such and kept small and separate.
- **Mr Market (Chapter 8).** Picture a business partner who quotes a price every day, euphoric one day and despairing the next. You may ignore him, deal only when his price suits you, and never treat his quote as a verdict on value.
- **Margin of safety (Chapter 20).** The amount by which intrinsic value exceeds price. Its job is to make an accurate forecast of the future unnecessary. It exists only at a price: a great business can offer none.

**Buffett and owner earnings.** Berkshire's 1986 letter defines owner earnings as reported earnings plus depreciation, depletion, amortisation and other non-cash charges, minus the average annual capital spending needed to maintain the business's competitive position and unit volume, plus any extra working capital that requires. Buffett says the spending term is necessarily a guess, and that this figure, not GAAP earnings, is the relevant one for valuation. The 2023 letter makes the same point for BNSF: it must spend more than its depreciation just to hold its position.

**Klarman.** *Margin of Safety* (1991) treats the cushion as protection against human error, bad luck and an unpredictable world. It puts loss avoidance ahead of relative performance. His forced-seller lesson also cuts the other way: sell when you want to, not when you have to.

Evidence: the margin-of-safety idea is a risk-control principle rather than a testable claim. The broad value premium is Moderate and contested, and it has varied by market and decade.

**For us:**
- Give every Core holding a conservative value range, not a point target, and measure the margin against the low end.
- A falling price is not a margin of safety. Buying because it fell is already on his failure-mode list.
- Owner earnings is the right lens for the Vedanta entities and ADANIPOWER, where sustaining capex is large and lumpy and EBITDA overstates what owners can take out. It is the wrong lens for Indian Bank (Section 6).
- Klarman's forced-seller point applies to us as the seller. Margin against pledged stock can make us the forced seller at the worst price.

## 2. Quality, moats and growth
**Buffett and Munger.**
- **Moats.** The 2007 letter describes a great business as one with a durable advantage that protects a high return on invested capital. Competitors attack any profitable castle; low-cost production (GEICO, Costco) and strong global brands (Coca-Cola, Gillette, American Express) are his examples of barriers. A moat that has to be rebuilt constantly is not a moat.
- **Circle of competence.** The 1996 letter says you need to evaluate only a few businesses, not many. The size of the circle matters less than knowing where its edge is.
- **Munger.** *Poor Charlie's Almanack* (2005, edited by Peter Kaufman) collects his talks. Two habits stand out: a latticework of mental models drawn from many fields, and inversion (from the mathematician Jacobi): ask what would guarantee failure, then avoid it. Jason Zweig reported in the Wall Street Journal that Munger called "sitting on your ass" one of the keys to good results: do nothing most of the time and act with aggression when bargains abound.

**Fisher.** *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits* (1958) gives 15 points that mix business traits (growth runway, margins, R&D) with management traits (integrity, depth, candour with investors when things go badly). "Scuttlebutt" means gathering facts from customers, suppliers, competitors, former employees and trade sources, not just from the company. He wanted a few outstanding holdings kept for a long time.

**India.**
- **Raamdeo Agrawal and QGLP.** Quality, Growth, Longevity, Price. Motilal Oswal's Wealth Creation Study is now in its 30th edition (2020-25, published December 2025). It reports that the top 100 wealth creators added INR 148 trillion, a 38% CAGR against 21% for the Sensex. Bharti Airtel (INR 7.9 trillion) and ICICI Bank (INR 7.4 trillion) were the largest, BSE the fastest, HAL the most consistent. Outlook Money notes that the March 2020 base was unusually low. The 29th study (2019-24) named Reliance the largest, Adani Green the fastest, and used the theme "bruised blue chips". Evidence: Anecdotal. These studies are descriptive, look back at winners and are sensitive to the start date. They show what compounders looked like, not how to find them in advance.
- **Saurabh Mukherjea and Coffee Can Investing** (2018, with Rakshit Ranjan and Pranab Uniyal; the earlier *The Unusual Billionaires* is the source). The screen asks for ROCE of at least 15% and revenue growth of at least 10% in every one of ten years, then a ten-year hold. Secondary sources disagree on the growth threshold (some say 15%). Evidence: the authors' own back-test; I found no independent test. The screen has no price term.
- **Vijay Kedia and SMILE.** Small in size, Medium in experience, Large in aspiration, Extra-large in market potential. He wants clean managements with a 15-year runway, pays little attention to ratios such as ROCE, and will buy something he expects not to move for six months (Bloomberg Quint, about 2018). Evidence: Anecdotal.
- **Chandrakant Sampat (1929-2015).** Often called India's first value investor. A PPFAS profile says he held multinationals such as Hindustan Lever and Gillette India for decades, favoured low capex and ROCE of 25% or more, and bought in the 1970s when FERA pushed foreign parents to dilute. Damani and Parag Parikh have credited him as an influence. Evidence: Anecdotal.
- **Radhakishan Damani and Rakesh Jhunjhunwala.** Secondary sources describe Damani as patient, contrarian and concentrated (fewer than 15 stocks, in one account), anchored by Avenue Supermarts (DMart, listed 2017). Jhunjhunwala is credited with early, very large Titan and Crisil positions, buying Titan while it struggled, long holds through downturns and heavy reading. Widely repeated quotes from both are unverified and not used. Evidence: Anecdotal.
- **Sanjay Bakshi** (MDI Gurgaon; ValueQuest Capital, moats investing) teaches behavioural finance, valuation and "Financial Shenanigans and Governance", and credits Munger's multidisciplinary models. **Basant Maheshwari** wrote *The Thoughtful Investor*; I could source only its themes (risk management, stock selection, behavioural finance, portfolio construction) and a call for patience.

**For us:**
- The question is "good, and at what price". Agrawal's P and Marks's second-level thinking (Section 3) are the guard against paying anything for quality.
- For the metals, power and PSU-bank names, the moat is mostly cost position or licence, not brand. Ask where each Vedanta entity sits on its own cost curve. A commodity producer in the bottom quartile of the cost curve is a different holding from one in the top.
- ETERNAL, ICICIGI and JIOFIN fit the quality lens better. Fisher would ask about management candour and reinvestment runway, not only the multiple.

## 3. Reading what the price implies, and second-level thinking
**Mauboussin.** *Expectations Investing* (Rappaport and Mauboussin, 2001; revised 2021) starts from the share price and solves backwards for the sales growth, margins and investment the price requires (price-implied expectations, a reverse DCF). The next question is whether those expectations are likely to be revised up or down. Their site offers a free reverse-DCF spreadsheet.

**Marks.** Second-level thinking is the first chapter of *The Most Important Thing* (2011) and grew out of his 2006 memo "Dare to Be Great". A first-level thinker sees a good company and buys. A second-level thinker asks what everyone already believes and what is different about their own view. His example: the outlook is bad, but everyone is selling in panic, so buy. His 2014 follow-up asks whether you are willing to be different and willing to be wrong.

**Damodaran.** *Narrative and Numbers* (2017) argues that value comes from a story tied to numbers (growth, margins, reinvestment, risk) and numbers tied to a story. Its cases include Uber and the Brazilian miner Vale, used to show how country, commodity and currency shape a company's story. I recall his test as possible, plausible and probable; I did not re-verify it.

Evidence: reverse-DCF is a method, not a return claim; it disciplines the question without answering it.

**For us:**
- The Playbook already asks "What is already priced in?" and "Where is our variant perception?" A reverse-DCF makes those answerable in one line.
- Earnings/revision momentum is Mauboussin's loop: price moves when expectations are revised, not when results are merely good.
- IDBI shows the point. Press reports on 14 Oct had the shares up 13% to Rs 93.62, above the reported Rs 81 deal price. The market is pricing something beyond the headline offer.

Proposal for the weekly review: add a one-line "price implies" field (growth, margin, years) to each Core and Valuation Rotation Candidate row in the Portfolio Book, refreshed at the monthly IC.

## 4. Special situations and spin-offs
**Greenblatt.** *You Can Be a Stock Market Genius* (1997) covers spin-offs, restructurings, merger securities, rights offerings, recapitalisations, bankruptcies and risk arbitrage. The spin-off case rests on two mechanics: the new company is under-followed, and holders of the parent who did not want it sell regardless of value. Klarman describes spin-offs as often an unloved child (low return on assets, litigation or weak management) and adds forced selling by index deletions and rating downgrades.

**Evidence on spin-offs.**
- Cusatis, Miles and Woolridge (1993, *Journal of Financial Economics*): 1965-88 abnormal returns for spun-off firms, parents and the pair, concentrated in firms involved in takeovers.
- McConnell, Ozbilgin and Wahal (2001, *Journal of Business*): a strategy that could have been traded in real time was about break-even against matched benchmarks.
- McConnell and Ovtchinnikov (2004): spun-off shares beat benchmarks over 36 months, 1965-2000 (I saw only an excerpt). A later European study found no significant long-run abnormal returns.
- India: event studies measure announcement windows, not multi-year returns. One study of 24 demergers (2009-10 to 2018-19) found cumulative abnormal returns of about 5.1% and 6.6% for two windows around the announcement. A Strides Pharma case found no significant effect.
- Rating: Moderate for announcement effects; Weak and contested for a tradeable long-run edge; India long-run not established.

**Magic Formula.** *The Little Book That Beats the Market* (2005) ranks stocks on cheapness (EBIT to enterprise value) and return on capital and buys the top ranks. The book's back-test is cited at about 30.8% a year. Alpha Architect (2011) found outperformance but far less, and sensitive to small method changes. Gray and Carlisle's *Quantitative Value* reports, in its own back-test, that cheapness alone did better. A Norwegian back-test (2003-22) found monthly alpha of about 0.5%, significant before costs and marginal after. I found no Indian test. Rating: Moderate for the cheap-and-profitable idea; Weak for the exact formula.

**For us:**
- **Vedanta.** Per press reports, the demerger used a record date of 1 May 2026 (ex-date 30 April) and a 1:1 ratio into four new entities (Aluminium, Power, Oil and Gas, Iron and Steel); Vedanta Ltd keeps base metals including the Hindustan Zinc stake. Announcement-window gains, where they exist, accrue at announcement, before listing. For an existing holder the live question is not "buy the spin-off" but that we now hold five separate businesses, each with its own cycle, debt and governance, and each needs its own label and thesis. The "rebuild from cash today" test should be run entity by entity. Open questions: how debt was split, how each is valued against peers, and whether index or mandate selling is still an overhang. NSE changed its index-inclusion rules for spun-off entities in 2023; check the current treatment.
- **IDBI (Hypothesis Lab, not a holding).** Press reports in mid-October say the cabinet approved Fairfax at Rs 81 a share for the combined 60.72% (government 30.48%, LIC 30.24%; about Rs 53,000 crore). RBI clearance is pending and an open offer to minorities follows signing. Reports conflict on how formal the approval is. Use probability branches: closes on terms, delays or reprices, falls through. The base rate matters: Business Standard reported in March 2026 that the sale was likely scrapped because bids were below reserve, before it was revived. Public float is only about 5% (Moneylife).
- The Magic Formula is a screen for ideas, not a sizing tool, and not a setup family he is studying.

## 5. Cycles, commodities and cyclicals
**Marks.** In *Mastering the Market Cycle* (2018) the claim is that you cannot forecast the cycle but can judge where you stand in it and adjust. His Oaktree memo "The Pendulum" says investor sentiment rarely rests at the midpoint; it swings between greed and fear. He calls the credit cycle the most powerful and volatile force (publisher's blurb).

**Lynch.** In *One Up on Wall Street* (1989), cyclicals (autos, airlines, steel) are the category where the usual error is to treat them like stalwarts and hold through the downturn. From my recall of the book, the P/E is misleading: it looks lowest near peak earnings. Not re-verified.

**Templeton.** The best time to buy is the point of maximum pessimism, though it is unclear whether he coined the phrase. Morningstar reports that in September 1939 he bought 100 shares each of 104 stocks at USD 1 or less, and only four proved worthless. The rule is about price against value and the crowd's state, not about any fall.

**Graham** (from recall): use earnings averaged over a cycle, not the latest peak.

**For us:**
- The Vedanta cluster is a cycle bet on aluminium, zinc, oil and gas, steel and power, plus INR. Ask what the market capitalises: spot, or a mid-cycle price. Value on through-cycle earnings and sustaining capex (Section 1).
- A "Valuation Rotation Candidate" cyclical should be judged by earnings peak and cycle position, not the P/E alone.
- The same applies to MU in the US book (memory is a classic cycle). Marks's questions about where we are in the credit window bear on the Hypothesis Lab's AI-infrastructure credit beta row.
- Templeton does not license "buying because it fell". Maximum pessimism is evidence only if value, balance sheet and survival check out.

Proposal for the weekly review: for each cyclical Core holding, record the cycle position (trough, mid, peak) and the price assumed by the market, next to the label.

## 6. PSU banks and financials
- **Credit cycle.** Marks's recurring theme is that lenders look best when credit is easy and losses are low, which is also when the worst loans are made. This is a pattern in his memos; I did not re-verify a specific one. Read bank earnings as cycle-dependent.
- **Valuation anchor.** Banks are valued on price to book against sustainable return on equity and through-cycle credit cost, not on owner earnings.
- **Government as owner.** The minimum public shareholding deadline for PSU banks was reported extended to 1 Aug 2026; I could not verify what happened since. Press reports from 2025 described OFS and QIP plans for several PSBs. IDBI's sale, if it completes, shows the government will give up control of a bank.

**For us:**
- Indian Bank is about 23% of the Indian book and a main piece of collateral. The masters' questions are: what does it earn at a normal credit cost, what ROE is sustainable, how large is the government's stake and the dilution supply overhang, and what is the bond-book sensitivity after the RBI hike to 5.50% on 7 Oct. I do not have current figures for these.
- Metals and PSU banks both depend on domestic cyclicality and foreign flows, so they are less diversifying than they look.
- The thesis should survive being wrong on timing. If it needs a re-rating by a particular date, it is a catalyst trade and should be labelled and sized as one, not as Core.

## 7. Concentration versus diversification, and sizing
**For concentration.** Pabrai's *The Dhandho Investor* (2007) has a chapter titled "Few Bets, Big Bets, Infrequent Bets" and a treatment of the Kelly formula. Its "low risk, high uncertainty" idea is to buy where uncertainty is high but the range of outcomes, conservatively handicapped, has limited downside. Fisher wanted a few outstanding holdings. Damani and Jhunjhunwala are reported to have run concentrated books (Section 2).

**Counterweight.** Graham asked the defensive investor to diversify. Bogle's case for the index fund rests on most people having no edge to concentrate.

**What the concentrators have in common:** deep knowledge, a margin of safety, bets that are independent of each other, and no forced selling. Kelly sizing depends on an edge and odds you must estimate, and those estimates are noisy. Overbetting costs more than underbetting; this is a general property of Kelly, not from one source.

**For us:**
- Thirteen stocks do not mean thirteen bets. The five Vedanta tickers share a promoter and a commodity cycle; Indian Bank adds a similar domestic-cycle and foreign-flow exposure. Together they are about 56% of the Indian book (23% plus about 33%) and over half of the pledged collateral. The US book is about 56% AI hardware and datacentre.
- By the masters' test the cluster fails on independence and on forced selling, even if each name passes on knowledge.
- Valuation-dislocation ideas are funded from cash, in stages, per the Playbook. That matches Klarman and Pabrai: size up only when the margin is large and survival is not in doubt.

Proposal for the weekly review: add a "cluster" column to the Portfolio Book and show cluster weights, and each cluster's share of collateral, in the monthly IC and the Collateral and Stress Test.

## 8. When to sell and rotate
**Fisher** gives three reasons to sell: you made a mistake; the company no longer meets the criteria, usually because management or growth prospects deteriorated; or a clearly better use of the money exists. He warns against selling just because a market correction is expected, or because the stock looks somewhat overvalued. A summary of the book reports his line that, if the purchase was done right, the time to sell is almost never.

**Lynch** says the category decides the exit. Sources disagree on exact thresholds; treat them as approximate.

**The "rebuild from cash" test.** It is the practical form of Fisher's third reason: would I buy this position, at this size, today, with the after-tax cash I would receive from selling it? Tax and trading costs matter here (Bogle). If the answer is "yes, but smaller", that is a sizing signal, which means rotation by trimming. Run it on each Vedanta entity separately.

Proposal for the weekly review: add a "Lynch category" field to the Portfolio Book and make every Valuation Rotation Candidate review state the category-specific exit signal and the after-tax rebuild answer.

## 9. Promoter quality, governance and pledging in India
- **Management quality.** Fisher's points on integrity and candour; Kedia's insistence on clean managements with a fire in the belly; Bakshi's course on financial shenanigans and governance. Kedia says cheap stocks are often cheap because the management is weak.
- **Pledging evidence (Moderate).** A 2024 study of 257 BSE 500 firms over 2011-20 (Emerald, DOI 10.1108/JAMR-01-2023-0003) finds pledging linked with higher future crash risk and weaker future financial performance. A 2026 conference paper using Prowess data for 2009-23 finds heavier pledging goes with more downside risk. The mechanism: a falling price shrinks collateral, lenders make margin calls, and if the promoter cannot meet them the lenders sell, which pushes the price down further. These are observational studies, so they show association, not proof of cause.
- **Disclosure.** Under SEBI's takeover regulations (Regulation 31) promoters must disclose encumbrances, invocations and releases, within seven working days. SEBI reads "encumbrance" widely, including non-disposal undertakings; a 2020 order penalised two Yes Bank promoter entities Rs 1 crore on this basis.
- **A checklist (my synthesis, not one source):** pledge level and trend and its reason; cash upstreamed to the parent through dividends and fees; related-party flows; unrelated capital allocation; auditor changes; cash flow against reported profit.

**For us:**
- Vedanta reports in July 2026 described encumbrances over about 54.7% of Vedanta Ltd tied to bond financing (USD 1.75 billion, with a covenant to keep at least 50.1%) and a separate USD 1 billion facility, and an August report said all encumbrances were released from 21 Aug 2026. The reports disagree on share counts and sequence. Read the latest shareholding and SAST filings.
- If the promoter group borrows against the same stock we post as collateral, two claimants stand on one asset. In a drawdown, a promoter margin call could depress the price just as our own margin tightens. Governance risk and funding risk are the same event.
- Governance is a gate. Failing it means a smaller size or no position, not a lower price target.

## 10. Temperament and process
- **Graham:** Mr Market is a service, not a guide. **Munger:** invert, and wait. **Marks:** be willing to be wrong and different, and hold forecasts with humility.
- **Bogle** (*The Little Book of Common Sense Investing*, 2007): costs compound against you; his "costs matter" argument is arithmetic. In SPIVA India year-end 2025, 75.0% of active large-cap funds trailed their benchmark over one year, 74.2% over three, 84.4% over five and 76.3% over ten. Active mid- and small-cap funds did better in 2025. Evidence: Moderate to Strong for Indian large-cap funds (repeated editions); weaker for mid- and small-caps. Stock-picking by an individual is a different test, but the benchmark is still the honest alternative.
